**Key measurements of soil health:**

biological (organisms living in soil), chemical (nutrients and pH), and physical (structure and water movement).  
 Physical:

* Bulk Density: Indicates compaction and porosity; higher densities can restrict root growth.  
* Infiltration Rate: Measures how quickly water moves into the soil, influencing runoff and moisture availability.  
* Available Water Capacity: Measures the soil’s ability to hold water for plant use.

Chemical:

* Soil pH: Measures acidity/alkalinity, affecting nutrient availability.  
* Nutrient Levels (P, K, N): Assesses fertility (e.g., Phosphorus, Potassium, Nitrogen).  
* Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC): Measures the soil’s capacity to hold onto vital nutrients.

Biological:

* Active Carbon (Active C): Measures the small portion of organic matter that provides an immediate food source for microbes.  
* Autoclaved Citrate Extractable (ACE) Protein: Measures nitrogen-rich proteins that are available to microorganisms.  
* Soil Respiration: Measures CO2 production by microorganisms, indicating microbial metabolism and activity levels.


**How does soil health impact agriculture**  
Healthier soil can grow healthier crops with fewer inputs and can withstand strong weather conditions better. Additionally, poorer quality soil compacts, erodes, and locks up nutrients. As such farmers are forced to compensate with more fertilizer, which can worsen the underlying problem over time as well as contribute to exogenous environment degradation.

**Identify universal good signs of soil**  
Dark color with earthy smell – which is caused by microbial activity – is the most rudimentary test. Fast-draining water, visible earthworms, and soil that holds together in loose clumps when squeezed are all reliable indicators as well. These align closely with the measurable indicators but don't require any equipment. Obviously, high/low indicators of the aforementioned indicators of soil health are more concrete indicators of health. 

**What data does farmers actually have**  
Most farmers have basic soil test results (pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) and their own yield records. But the more sensitive indicators — microbial communities, aggregate stability, infiltration rates, CEC — require specialized testing that's expensive and not routine.For example, some farmers have chemical tests of soil health such as Protozoa, a microbe beneficial to soil health. As such, this means most farms are managed off incomplete information, and the biology is almost always the unknown variable.
